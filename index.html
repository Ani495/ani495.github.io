Introduction

An X–Y pedestal moves a dish to follow a satellite across the sky. The common azimuth over elevation layout has a singularity overhead, so a fast satellite near zenith forces the mount to whip through it. An X–Y mount does not, which is why it suits low orbits.

My scope is the mechanism: structure, bearings, drivetrain, balance and load path. The reflector, RF hardware and tracking software belong to other people, so to me the payload is a mass, a CG and a mounting interface. Nothing is built yet; this is CAD, analysis and supplier correspondence.

The Pointing Requirement

The requirement starts from signal loss, not angle. Total pointing error has to cost less than half a decibel at the highest transmit frequency, and working that backwards gives 0.1 degrees. It scales with aperture, since a larger dish has a narrower beam and gets a tighter budget.

Travel is ±180 degrees on both axes, ideally with no mechanical limit. Ground slope at a deployment site cannot be predicted, so the margin has to be in the machine rather than assumed away.

Requirement chain Beamwidth ≈ 70 λ/D → 0.50° at Ka band on 1.35 m
Loss ≈ 12 (Δθ/θ3dB)² → 0.48 dB at 0.1°
Travel: ±180° both axes, not mechanically limited

How the Design Got Here

Four versions, and each change came from learning something the version before it had not accounted for. Read top to bottom, it is mostly the story of one dimension, the distance between the two axes, getting smaller for reasons that were not the ones I started with.

01August

Fork, trunnions, axes 800 mm apart

A fork and yoke, chosen over a ring because the travel requirement rules out anything that encloses the dish. Trunnion shafts ran along the upper axis behind the dish, and both axes were supported at both ends. The axes sat 800 mm apart, a figure I had taken as a clearance floor from the dish's own sweep radius.

What changed it: I drew the full swept volume in CAD. Nothing interfered at 365 mm, so the floor had never been real.

02Early September

Pedestal, 365 mm offset

With the floor gone the offset came down to 365 mm and the machine got about 430 mm shorter. The upper axis should sit at the payload's centre of mass so the dish balances about it, but that station is where the feed horn lives, so the dish was stood off on a pedestal to clear it.

What changed it: the upper axis no longer passed through the centre of mass, so its drive carried a standing gravity load before any wind blew.

03September 8

Upper axis on the centre of mass

The direct fix was to bring the upper axis forward onto the centre of mass, with a cavity in the backing ring so the feed could nest inside it. That balanced the upper axis completely. I built the variants alongside the pedestal and compared them side by side.

What changed it: keeping the dish clear of the lower drive forced the offset well beyond the pedestal's, and the machine got taller. Balancing one axis cost the dimension that matters most, so it was rejected.

04September 10 to now

No trunnions, 250 mm offset

A larger offset was mechanically fine but hurt control. The upper assembly swings about the lower axis on that offset, so every extra millimetre adds inertia, and any imbalance on the upper axis turns lower axis motion into torque on the upper one in proportion to it. A smaller offset keeps the two closer to independent loops and pushes the drive's resonance higher, at the price of inertia that had been rejecting wind for free.

So the redesign went after the offset directly. The trunnion shafts are gone and the fork carries the dish, the lower drive turns a flat plate the upper axis sits on, the backing ring is my own design at 4.2 kg, and the far side of the upper axis floats. The offset is 250 mm. It is not the lever arm the wind acts through, which runs from the lower axis to the dish and still sets the drivetrain load.

Still moving: the fork has gone from 20 to 73 Hz, and the mass properties of this build come next.

Building the Error Budget

No allocation had been issued telling me how much of that tenth of a degree the mechanism could consume, so I wrote it myself: a twelve-row RSS error budget taking half the system total, with the bottom-up sum closing inside it. I did not assume every row root sum squares, since that is only valid for independent zero mean errors. Gravity sag and steady wind act through the same compliance in the same direction and add linearly, and taking the pessimistic reading of every correlated pair busts the budget by about 1.6 times.

What it showed changed what I worked on next.

What the budget showed 78% of pointing error enters downstream of the encoder, so no loop gain reaches it
Control touches 1/5 of total variance. A flawless controller buys a few mdeg; stiffness is a 4× larger lever
Velocity feedforward is mandatory. On feedback alone, tracking lag eats the entire budget from one row
Row inverted to a hardware spec: 0.28 mm max rim deflection at the design wind case

Checking that stiffness target against a standard aluminium box section showed roughly twice the second moment of area needed, so I expected the structural risk to sit in the bearings and joints. The FEA later put most of the fork's compliance in its legs and top.

Drivetrain Sizing

Before any hardware was bought I checked whether the specified drive could hold the machine still at the design wind case. It cannot, and reaching a number I trust took two corrections.

The first estimate imported a wind torque figure I had been given. Tracing it back showed it already contained the weight of the machine it was measured on, and I was adding our own gravity on top, so weight was counted twice. I stopped importing torque at all. The load is now the CFD force on the dish, which a drag and lift solve cannot contaminate with weight, multiplied by our own measured lever arm.

The second was a pose the machine cannot occupy. The old sum added the worst force, with the dish face on, to the worst torque at a different angle. Summing each angle with itself and taking the worst removed that conservatism.

Neither correction saved the drive. The gearbox survives the design case at about 90 percent of its rating, but the drive behind it delivers well short of the hold, and aerospace practice asks for a 2:1 torque margin on top. The drive offers about a third of that. A larger gearbox frame does not fix it: output torque is motor torque times ratio times efficiency, frame size appears nowhere in that product, and the larger frame carries the same ratio ceiling. A larger motor closes the gap, and a direct drive torque motor is the alternative I have not evaluated yet.

The simulation did not catch any of this. The saturation check was inert and a later audit showed no code path read the drivetrain torque figures at all, so I found it on paper. Now I check an actuator can hold a disturbance before analysing how well a controller rejects it.

Static hold, lower axis, measured lever arm Required at design wind case: 372 N-m
Delivered at ratio ceiling: 257 N-m
Required with 2:1 margin: 744 N-m → about 1/3 delivered
Gearbox rating used: ~90%, and over rating at the higher wind case

Mass Properties

I built the payload mass model in Fusion 360 and checked the CG two independent ways rather than trusting one: a direct mass weighted summation over the bodies, then a back derivation from the assembly inertia tensor by parallel axis. They agree to half a millimetre.

That check found what earlier calculations had missed. Every previous balance calculation was one dimensional, justified by left to right symmetry, but symmetry only constrains one transverse axis and nobody had checked the other. A heavy component off the centreline breaks it, giving a 20.2 mm offset. The balance point also turned out far more sensitive to geometry than mass: feed and OMT mass across its range moves the axis about 8 mm, while swapping the optics moves it around 182 mm. That factor of twenty reversed which question I asked the programme first.

The payload has changed once since. I replaced the backing ring with my own design and the payload dropped 4.83 kg. The old one was budgeted at 9 kg and mine weighs 4.2, so the drop matches to 0.03 kg and nothing else in the model moved.

Key Decisions

Axis topology

Chose a fork and yoke over a ring and cradle. The travel spec drove it. A ring encloses the dish, so it cannot permit a full flip without absurd oversizing, and "ideally not mechanically limited" rules it out entirely. The fork's open geometry gives unrestricted travel, and its CG imbalance is bounded work, not a reason to reconsider.

Gravity balance

Chose an adjustable counterweight over a zero-length spring counterbalance. The spring is elegant, matching gravity exactly at every elevation angle and saving roughly 30 percent of machine mass. Three costs killed it: it halves axis inertia, it turns a static base load into a fully reversing one, and it stores over a kilojoule in a machine serviced outdoors by hand.

Counterweight geometry

The balance requirement fixes a product, mass multiplied by arm length, so the arm length is free. Mass falls as one over distance while its contribution to inertia rises linearly, meaning a longer arm helps twice: it halves the counterweight and reduces the stiffness the axis needs. The redesign added a catch. A shorter machine leaves less room to swing the counterweight, so arm length became a trade against ground clearance rather than a free win.

Upper axis support

The fork sits in slots on both sides of the upper drive, so the axis is supported at both ends rather than cantilevered. The driven side is located by the gearbox's own main bearing, which already fixes the axis angle by itself. A second rigid bearing on the far side would over constrain it, fighting that alignment and binding as the structure grows with temperature.

So the far side floats: self aligning, free to slide axially, and with zero radial play, because any clearance there lets the dish tip where the encoder cannot see it. That bearing is not selected yet. The failure mode I worry about is duty rather than load, since a tracker spends its life dithering through tiny angles on the same rolling elements.

Structural section

Chose a hollow box with ribs over a three dimensional lattice. A lattice looks efficient until you remember these are bending members, and bending stress lives at the extreme fibre, so interior material is along for the ride. The limit on shelling it out further is local buckling and panel drumming rather than strength.

Structure and Modes

The acceptance test is the first mode that tips the dish, not the first mode anywhere. A lug or panel ringing on its own does not move the beam. The target is a control convention, about three times the upper-axis velocity loop, which puts it between roughly 60 and 80 Hz depending on how the upper axis is counterweighted.

The first grounded run of the whole load path put that mode at 20 Hz, three to four times short. I stiffened the fork over several iterations to 73 Hz. At 60 Hz, a diagnostic run with the base clamped showed about 87 percent of the remaining compliance sat in the legs and top, which told me where the next change belonged.

The gearbox is also a spring, and its torsional stiffness sits in series with the fork. By 73 Hz it had become the softer of the two, so the system mode is set mostly by the drive, and further stiffening of the fork buys about one hertz. The real inertia can only push the drive mode lower, so the conclusion holds while the number waits on the new mass properties.

73 Hz is also a ceiling. The model bonds every contact, which makes bolted corners perfectly rigid, and the gearbox compliance is not in the FEA at all.

Two runs that looked like answers

One early run returned 784 Hz. Nothing heavy was attached and single lugs were ringing on their own; frequencies arriving in near identical pairs were the tell. In another, a change had disconnected the dish and a 157 Hz mode looked like the answer, but it was the empty machine. Neither frequency meant anything until I looked at the mode shape.

Two Results I Had to Retract

A bandwidth figure that was a solver artifact

I closed out a disputed control bandwidth figure and got agreement with the published number to within a third of a percent. A match that clean made me suspicious, so I refined the solver grid, and the value drifted monotonically away as the grid got finer. The agreement was a quantisation artifact: the pipeline scans candidate points and takes the first that passes rather than solving for the crossing. I reverted my own published closure and re-derived it by bisection, which reproduced an earlier outside audit exactly.

A stability limit that was wrong by two orders of magnitude

Every control loop had been designed against the wrong transfer function. The loop closes on what the encoder reads, and with a load-side encoder the structural compliance sits downstream of both actuator and sensor, making it collocated and benign rather than a stability hazard. Correcting it moved the stability limit by about 200 times.

I propagated that correction despite an unwelcome consequence. With collocation accounted for, a single-mode model imposes no bandwidth limit at all, so it cannot determine the stiffness requirement, and the first mode figure the project had been quoting was convention rather than output.

Current Status

Nothing is fabricated and there is no test data. The machine was redesigned in September around the smaller axis offset. The drivetrain and structural figures above use that build's measured geometry; the error budget and the original mass model predate it. Still open: mass properties for the new build, which set both axis inertias and every bandwidth figure downstream; the drive, which needs a larger motor and an outdoor rating the current gearbox's catalogue explicitly excludes; and the counterweights, which are not yet drawn.

A configuration audit also found most analysis parameters were inert, set in the config and read by nothing. A parameter nothing reads still looks set, so it gives a silent wrong answer.

Introduction

RobotX is an international competition in which an unmanned surface vessel, an underwater vehicle and a UAV have to cooperate as one system to complete storm response tasks. The 2026 edition is the first that requires all three domains working together rather than independently.

The requirements are published, dimensioned and written by someone else. Payload masses, target sizes, signalling states and visibility distances all come from the rulebook, which is about as close as a student project gets to a customer.

I worked on four subsystems. Three of them were built.

The Test Vessel

Before the autonomy work could go on the competition boat we needed somewhere to bring the stack up where a failure cost us nothing important, so we built one out of a bodyboard with four thrusters. It floats, it is roughly the right scale, and nothing on it is precious.

It runs ArduRover on a Pixhawk, which is the rover and boat firmware variant rather than the copter one, because a surface vessel's control problem is not a multirotor's, with a Raspberry Pi alongside as the mission computer. It completed autonomous waypoint missions on the water.

To be clear about who did what: a teammate configured the Raspberry Pi and the ArduPilot side. My part was the vehicle itself, meaning the hull, the thrusters and the mounting that packages the flight controller, companion computer and power delivery onto one plate across two revisions, along with the architecture comparison below that the configuration was built on.

The platform also exercises thrust allocation, which I found useful. Four thrusters on a flat hull is more actuators than the degrees of freedom being commanded, so there is a mapping from a desired surge, sway and yaw down to individual thruster outputs. That is worth debugging somewhere cheap.

Subsystem Decisions

Control architectureTrade study

The split came from a documented comparison, and the two candidates fail for different reasons. A general-purpose microcontroller cannot be the flight controller: not enough memory for state estimation, no floating point unit, and a single sequential loop means one slow sensor read stalls everything behind it. A Linux companion board cannot either, for a different reason. It has plenty of compute but is not a real-time system, so timing jitter at loop rate is unbounded.

Because they fail differently the answer is both, with the work divided. Dedicated flight controller for vehicle control, Linux companion for the mission layer, two failure domains that do not share hardware.

UAV payload gripperCAD only

Tasks 2 and 3 need the UAV to retrieve a specific coloured tin from a floating platform and deliver it to a specific coloured circle. The tins are 40 to 50 grams. I designed a four prong gripper driven from a single servo through a self-locking worm rather than a stalled servo grasp.

A conventional gripper holds by commanding a stalled position, so it draws current the whole carry, heats the servo and drops the payload on any brownout. The worm holds at zero current and keeps the payload through complete power loss, which matters when the alternative is dropping it over water during a scored run. The right-angle geometry pays off again by letting the servo lie flat along the airframe instead of hanging into the downward camera view, and occlusion of that camera is a documented open problem here.

Self-locking is a condition rather than a property of worm drives generally. It holds when the lead angle falls below the friction angle for the material pair, which is why the tooth geometry and the choice between a printed and a purchased wheel are the same decision.

Fire suppression nozzleBuilt

Task 3 requires the vessel to spray water at a 25 cm target window until it changes colour. I designed and built the converging junction merging two feed lines into a single delivery line, plus the servo mounting module that lets the nozzle be aimed.

The aim angle comes from solving the jet's flight backwards. With a fixed exit velocity and a target at known height and standoff, you solve the projectile relation for the launch angle that lands on it. It is quadratic in tan θ, so there are two valid answers, a flat trajectory and a lobbed one. I took the flat one because it is less sensitive to standoff error, and standoff is what a vessel station-keeping in chop cannot promise.

I did not model drag, deliberately. Over this standoff the correction on a coherent jet is small compared to the target window and much smaller than the vessel's own position error, which decides where the stream lands.

Nothing goes to the printer before an interference and cross section check on the assembly, run against measured shrinkage rather than nominal dimensions. What I Would Check First on the Gripper

It is designed and not built, so I would rather state the weaknesses than have somebody find them.

Self-locking worm drives are efficiency poor, typically well under half, so the reduction is bought with heat and needs a larger servo than a spur gear gripper would. Whether that nets out positive depends on carry duration, and I have measured neither side. The mechanism also almost certainly outweighs its payload by a large multiple, which collides with the mass versus flight time tradeoff this vehicle is already constrained by. Mechanism mass as a fraction of all-up weight is the first number I would take, even if it is unflattering.

Four rigid fingers is over constrained. Three contact points fully constrain a cylinder, so one finger will not touch in practice, and first contact takes all the load and can walk the payload off centre. A compliant fingertip would conform instead of clamping. And the property that makes it safe under power loss makes it unforgiving under impact: no back drive means no manual release, so a hard landing puts the impact straight into the gear teeth and a jam is unrecoverable in the air.

Introduction

A team of four built a self-driving 1/10-scale RC car over one quarter. My work bridged both halves: designing every mount that holds the electronics to the chassis, then taking the car through the ROS2 stack until it drove the track on its own.

My role sat on the mechanical-to-software boundary. The vision pipeline only works if the camera sits at the right height and angle, and keeps working only if its cabling is clean and nothing rattles loose on lap one.

Designing the Mounts

I designed the full set of mounts in Onshape and printed them at the UCSD makerspace: main plate, camera mount with the LiDAR on top, Raspberry Pi and USB hub, DC-DC converter, anti-spark, relay and Arduino, GPS, and start button.

Base plate

Chose a breadboard plate on a 1 cm hole grid over a fixed layout designed around the components. The class moved fast, and components would get moved or swapped as we went, so a fixed layout would have meant reprinting the base every time. The grid let us rearrange the whole car in minutes with a screwdriver.

One detail made it work: every hole modelled at 3.5 mm rather than nominal to absorb FDM shrinkage, so M3 screws pass without tapping or forcing. Across a few hundred holes, that saved an afternoon of forcing screws.

Bringing the Car Up in ROS2

The goal was three clean autonomous laps following the track's yellow markers. I followed the course setup procedure, but our parts and software differed from the guide, so a good chunk of the work was diagnosing the gaps.

The whole stack runs in a Docker container on the Pi, which buys reproducibility: every team runs an identical environment and a broken setup is restored by re-pulling rather than reinstalling. The most important habit in the project turned out to be the build step, since ROS2 uses colcon and compiling alone is not enough. Every edit to a config, launch file or node has to be followed by a rebuild and re-source, or ROS2 keeps running the old values.

Foxy versus JazzyThe main hurdle

The course guide was written for ROS2 Foxy and we were running Jazzy, so packages the guide assumed were baked into the image were not there. I traced it to two missing dependencies: a multicam package the OAK-D Lite needed to publish its stream, and an Ackermann package required to configure the control nodes so the VESC could drive throttle and steering.

I found them by working out how the pieces of the stack depend on each other, not by retrying.

Power and network faults

Power. The OAK-D Lite draws more current at peak than the Pi's USB ports comfortably supply, which shows up as the camera randomly dropping out or feeding a black image. That looks exactly like a driver fault and is not one.

Network isolation. Every ROS2 robot on the same WiFi is visible to every other by default, so a calibration window can show another team's camera feed, or steering commands can drive someone else's car. Assigning each team a unique domain ID makes the robots invisible to each other at the network layer with no code changes at all.

Calibration and Tuning

All calibration was done with the car on the track, so what I tuned matched real conditions rather than a bench approximation. The pipeline converts each frame into HSV and keeps only the pixels inside a tuned window, so yellow markers turn white and everything else turns black. Lab lighting shifted through the day, so this was iterative. The values I landed on diverge from the guide defaults, notably a Hue floor of 13 against a suggested 25, because they were dialled in against our actual track.

Centreline and PID

The track guidance is dotted rather than continuous, so the system finds the marker blobs and computes a centreline to steer toward, with the difference from the camera centreline reference becoming the steering error. Tuning the contour filters and the error dead band turns a flickering set of dots into one stable target.

Too much proportional gain and the car oscillates down the straights, too little and it reacts late and slides off the curves. Throttle is scheduled against error too. I tuned it on the track, rebuilding and relaunching after each change.

Proportional0.400 → 0.765   nearly doubled, so the car responded firmly to error instead of correcting sluggishly into curves
Integral0.000 → 0.000   left at zero, no persistent one sided drift needed accumulating correction
Derivative0.100 → 0.162   raised to damp the oscillation the higher proportional gain introduced

Pushing proportional up makes the car more responsive and also more prone to weaving, so derivative had to come up alongside it. Integral stayed at zero, which means the steering was mechanically well centred and the centreline calibrated, with no systematic bias for an integral term to correct.

Results

The car completed all three autonomous laps cleanly and then kept going, lapping indefinitely without failing. Lapping past three is what showed the calibration and tuning were repeatable, not one lucky run.

Solving It Twice

Earlier in the course we drove the same car with a trained convolutional network instead of a hand built pipeline. That version used roughly 15,000 self collected images, trained on university GPU clusters over a remote session in under ten minutes, and was validated in simulation before it went near the physical car. It demonstrated three autonomous laps publicly at the Del Mar Electronics and Manufacturing Show, in front of industry visitors, on a track it had not seen before that day.

The network needed data and the pipeline needed understanding. Collecting fifteen thousand frames takes no knowledge of why the car turns, but every CV pipeline parameter had to be reasoned about, because nothing in it tunes itself. The pipeline also fails legibly, since when lane detection breaks I can watch the mask and know it is a Hue floor or a shadow, while a network driving badly usually means it saw something in training I did not notice.

Choosing for a competition tomorrow, I would take the classical pipeline for anything I have to debug trackside under time pressure, and the learned approach for anything whose rules I cannot write down. I can only tell which problem I have because I built both.

Introduction

We had to build a robot that picks up rings from a set region of the arena and places them on a central statue. As team lead I first worked with the team to generate concepts and pick one. After that I owned the claw subsystem, modelled the full arm in Fusion 360, and ran simulations in the arena to check the concept before anything was fabricated. The arm segment sizing itself was owned by other members of the team; I modelled those dimensions in Fusion and focused my own design work on the gripper.

Competition Constraints

Hard material and actuator limits shaped almost every decision that followed. Six motors were permitted, four high-torque and two low-torque, high-speed; we used four. Material for the robot itself was rationed to two sheets of acrylic and one of aluminium. And the robot had to fit a roughly fifteen-inch height limit, which put the arm segments at about seven inches each. Working inside a ration that tight changes what good practice looks like, because you can't iterate by cutting final parts.

Choosing the Concept

Base topology

Chose a stationary arm on a rotating base over a mobile platform carrying an extended hook. Required reach inside the arena was very limited, so mobility added complexity we did not need, and with the motor budget that tight we wanted every motor on the arm itself. It also cut the error sources the operator had to manage, since they only ever had to think about the arm's own movements.

Simulating Before Cutting

Each part was modelled individually in Fusion 360 then brought into a single assembly, which made it straightforward to check the gear pairings at each joint and verify the claw mesh sat where it needed to before committing to fabrication.

With the assembly complete I placed the arm into a Fusion model of the arena, rings around the perimeter and statue at the centre, to confirm the stationary arm could reach the full layout and that the geometry fit the envelope, all before any material was cut.

The full operating cycle came out of this as a four-pose sequence: rotate the base to line up with a ring, rotate toward the statue, extend the lower arm, and finally turn the claw to drop the ring. That sequence is what justified three arm motors plus a dedicated claw motor.

The Claw

Gripper principle

Chose one moving jaw against one fixed jaw over two moving jaws. At constant torque, driving both halves the gripping force, so keeping one fixed turns it into a rigid support and puts the whole motor output behind the jaw that moves. The ring is held by mechanical entrapment in the cavity between the two arms, not by squeezing.

Motor off, the claw sits open. Motor on, the moving jaw rotates roughly 45 degrees until it meets the fixed jaw, then holds until the geared motor is driven in reverse to release.

BOTH JAWS MOVING
= HALF THE GRIP FORCE
RETENTION BY ENTRAPMENT
45° CLOSING SWEEP
Motor selectionFound by testing

The first claw used a high-torque motor with sharp-edged jaws, and in testing it was wrong twice over: far too sensitive to control, and it kept breaking down. We had no datasheet torque figure for the motors, so with nothing to design against, iterative testing was the only method available.

Swapped to the low-torque, high-speed motor, which gave controllable actuation, and redesigned the jaws with curved rather than sharp edges. The 3:1 gear ratio never changed; only the motor and the geometry did.

Claw Analysis

I analysed the claw to check whether the moving arm could retain a ring by entrapment and resist the forces from robot motion, impacts and stacking. Because the claw does not rely on pinching, the analysis focused on two things: whether the inertial force on the ring during acceleration could force the claw open, and the bending stress in the claw arm.

Can the ring force the claw open

The gear ratio between the motor and the rotating claw is 3:1, so the stall torque at the arm is three times the motor's stall torque. Taking the worst case, which is the largest force the ring can exert, the arm holds the ring closed as long as that available torque exceeds the ring's inertial force acting at the jaw radius. Substituting the numbers gives a maximum acceleration of about 78 metres per second squared before the ring would force the claw open. That is high enough that normal robot motion should never open the claw.

Will the jaw deform

I also checked how much load the claw arm could take before deforming, by comparing the bending stress to the yield stress of acrylic. Using a bending model with the ring load applied at the jaw tip, the calculated bending stress came out at approximately 0.94 MPa. Acrylic yields at roughly 60.5 MPa, so the calculated stress is far below that and the claw arm has a large structural margin.

Both of these are hand calculations and both are predictions rather than measurements. To confirm them in the real world, the plan was to apply a gradually increasing force at the tip of the moving arm with a spring scale, perpendicular, to simulate worst case loading, and to run a retention test by shaking and tilting the robot with a ring secured in the cavity to check for back driving or slippage.

Claw analysis summary Gear ratio, motor to claw: 3:1
Max acceleration before the ring opens the claw: ≈78 m/s²
Calculated bending stress in the jaw: ≈0.94 MPa
Acrylic yield stress: ≈60.5 MPa
Implied factor of safety: ≈64

The Failure We Found

If the claw was opened too far, the moving jaw could rotate past its meshing range and destabilise the gears against each other, which rendered the claw unusable for the rest of testing. It was not a jam. The teeth walked out of engagement and would not walk back.

The fix was to add hard stoppers alongside the claw so the moving jaw physically cannot travel past the point where the gears stay properly meshed. If a position on a mechanism matters, something physical should define it, not the control signal, because software only knows where it commanded the mechanism to go.

Lessons Learned

Concentrating the motors on the arm paid off. Choosing a stationary arm with a rotating base let us cover the whole arena with only four of the six allowed motors, and it kept the operation simple, because the operator only had to reason about the arm's movements.

Gear size limited arm length, which I did not anticipate going in. Arm segments could not be made arbitrarily long, because higher gear ratios produced gears that were sometimes larger than the arm segment itself and needed extra stabilising brackets to hold each segment in place. That fed directly into the roughly seven inch segment length. It was the first design decision I remember being settled by something other than the number I expected.

With only two sheets of acrylic, checking reach, motion and fit in the Fusion arena model before cutting was the right risk reduction step, and no material went on a layout that couldn't reach the rings or statue.

Introduction

A chat session starts from nothing. You explain the project, and what you actually explain is your current understanding of it, so any error you happen to be holding gets handed back as fact. Then the session ends and it is gone.

On a long project that is a real problem, and I can put a number on it: over the X–Y positioner work, 33 figures changed after first being written down. Dish depth 225 to 326 mm. Focal length 506 to 349. A gearbox ratio 201 to 164. A control bandwidth 13.95 to 13.41 Hz. A counterweight requirement 72 to 91.7 kg-m. Every one was wrong for a while, and a fresh session would hand back whatever I last told it.

So I built a memory layer that reads from a record. Every figure carries where it came from, superseded values stay on the page marked as superseded rather than deleted, and retrieval flags them instead of serving them as current.

How It Got Here

Ten weeks, 378 logged sessions, about a dozen structural rewrites. Almost every upgrade came from measuring something rather than believing it, and several came from being told I was wrong.

Benchmarking the Memory

The open question was whether the system should become a typed knowledge graph, with atoms, typed edges and multi-hop traversal, or stay closer to plain prose retrieval. Rather than argue about it I built five architectures and measured them.

Thirty questions were generated blind from the source essays by an agent that never saw the engine, the rubrics were frozen before any arm ran, answering and grading were both blind, and all five arms routed through the identical 136-atom index so the only variable is what each does after routing.

Arm and behaviour after routingAccuracy   /   Tokens per question
Reads the whole matched page0.82   /   10,648
One best section per page0.87   /   2,580
Two best sections per page0.78   /   4,153
Typed traversal with a section lens0.80   /   2,536
Two sections plus typed paths, two hops0.76   /   5,275

The simplest arm scored highest at close to the lowest cost, 4.1 times cheaper than reading whole pages and no worse on answers. That's the opposite of what I expected going in. Everything I added lowered accuracy, and all but one addition raised cost.

Three isolations back that up. Typed edges against simply reading more prose lost 0.02 accuracy at 1.27 times the tokens. Two sections instead of one lost 0.09 at 1.61 times, because the second best section dilutes rather than informs. Atom-routed traversal against plain section search lost 0.07.

Why traversal lost

Traversal was not broken. It fired correctly on all thirty questions and produced valid chains. It lost because plain section search reached two or more documents on all eight multi-hop questions without using a single edge. A well posed question already contains the hop: ask why a given wind torque undermines a given motor and both ends are named, so ordinary search lands on both sections and traversal spends its budget re-deriving a link the question already made. Traversal only helps when the question cannot name the destination.

The atoms turned out not to be valuable as graph nodes but as an index. With 18 hand-written atoms the graph scored 0.39; with 136 auto-generated ones it scored 0.80, and every other arm improved too, purely because routing got better. Index quality lifted all five architectures and edges lifted none.

What I Removed

The consolidation feature

The last justification for typed edges was consolidation: finding rot nobody thought to query, such as a live conclusion resting on a retracted figure. I built it and tested it on the real vault. At full scale it returned 705 findings that were almost entirely extraction artifacts. Focused on 30 correction-dense pages it recovered 33 genuine superseded pairs and 4 real contradictions, but still found nothing new, because both contradictions were already written explicitly in the prose it had just read and the flagship capability fired zero times.

The reason is structural. This vault records what changed in detail and which claim rests on which figure almost never, so a stale dependency is undetectable without dependency structure. Consolidation re-surfaces documented rot rather than discovering hidden rot, so I removed it. The edges survive only as the staleness flag.

Three earlier versions of the benchmark

Before it stabilised, this benchmark gave three different answers. Auditing found why each one was wrong: keyword based grading that manufactured both a false win and a false loss, a strawman comparator that dumped whole files, baselines routed through a sparse index while the graph got a rich one, and a section lens that pasted the same text once per atom, wasting 21 percent of one arm's token budget.

The result only stabilised once every arm shared identical routing. It took three rebuilds to get arms that differ in exactly one thing.

The Personal Layer

The system has a personal layer as well as an engineering one, and the architecture above is applied to the engineering layer only, by design. Two independent reasons, either sufficient. Privacy: that layer never leaves the machine, so it is never decomposed by a delegated process. And narrative integrity: knowledge decomposes into claims and reflection does not, so a journal entry read back as a fragment loses the thread that was the actual content.

I drew that line deliberately and wrote down why.

Current Status

The system is in daily use. It holds 249 pages across concepts, entities and sources, 1,189 atoms and 378 logged sessions. It is the system holding the X–Y positioner project, and it wrote most of the source material behind this portfolio.

A session was loading roughly 17,700 tokens before it started thinking, up to 43,100 on a full load. Procedural and episodic memory are now split, with standing rules compiled into one 6.6 KB always-on file and the incident case files loaded only when writing a new rule.

On accuracy I want to be careful. The sample is 30 questions on one corpus, so the per category cells hold seven or eight each and 0.87 against 0.82 sits inside the noise. The claim I will defend is much cheaper and certainly no worse, not smarter. The cost result is large, consistent across all four categories, and the part I trust.
